\label{sec:proposal}

This chapter presents the core contribution of this research: a set of architectural guidelines for software startups adopting modular monolithic architectures. The guidelines are grounded in the gaps and trade-offs identified through the systematic literature review and reflect the architectural tensions of early-stage teams operating under limited resources, rapid iteration, and evolving requirements. They are framed as decision-making heuristics rather than a rigid prescriptive framework.

The research design builds on the twelve evaluation criteria identified through the systematic literature review, organized across four analytical dimensions introduced in Chapter~\ref{sec:relatedwork}: Architectural Design, Operational Fit, Organizational Alignment, and Guideline Orientation. This dissertation proposes six guidelines (G1--G6) that operationalize the first two dimensions, covering module boundaries, maintainability, progressive scalability, migration readiness, deployment strategy, and observability. The remaining two dimensions, Organizational Alignment and Guideline Orientation, are framed as future research (Section~\ref{sec:future-guidelines}).

\noindent
Figure~\ref{fig:guideline-dimensions} shows the four guideline dimensions used in this research to guide the evaluation of modular monolith architectures in startup environments:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=8pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Architectural Design.} Covers criteria related to the software's internal structure. Guideline G1 enforces clear module boundaries, G2 evaluates maintainability over time, and G3 designs for progressive scalability.

  \item \textbf{Operational Fit.} Addresses the operational requirements needed to support production environments. Guideline G4 checks migration readiness (for example, transitioning toward microservices), G5 focuses on deployment strategy and automation, and G6 considers observability levels (logging, metrics, monitoring).

  \item \textbf{Organizational Alignment.} A future research direction concerned with how module structure interacts with team organization, ownership models following Conway's Law~\cite{fowler2003}, DevOps maturity, and developer onboarding.

  \item \textbf{Guideline Orientation.} A future research direction concerned with how guidance itself should be packaged, including actionable patterns, context-sensitive recommendations, and explicit dilemmas rather than rigid rules.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Each dimension group provides a set of guidelines that, together, form the foundation for the proposal of this research. It illustrates how technical, operational, organizational, and orientation-based considerations should be integrated when selecting or evolving a modular monolith architecture in software startups. What follows is a descriptive presentation of the guidelines within each dimension and their foundation concepts, starting with those that address the architectural design of modular monoliths.

\section{Future Research}
\label{sec:future-guidelines}

This dissertation proposes six guidelines (G1--G6) that operationalize the Architectural Design and Operational Fit dimensions. The remaining two dimensions, Organizational Alignment and Guideline Orientation, are framed as future research because they require empirical methods beyond code-level analysis, including organizational case studies, practitioner surveys, and longitudinal team observations. The open questions in each dimension are summarized below as a research agenda rather than as pre-committed guidelines. Narrowing the operational set from twelve to six guidelines is a deliberate scope decision: committing all four dimensions inside a single master's research cycle would compromise the depth of the code-level verification that G1 to G6 require, so the deferred dimensions are kept as an explicit research agenda instead of being operationalized prematurely.

\subsection*{Organizational Alignment}

Open questions in this dimension include:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=8pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item How should module boundaries map to team boundaries following Conway's Law~\cite{fowler2003}, and what code ownership policies, module stewardship models, and accounting of cross-module change cost best support that mapping?
  \item What DevOps practices are required to sustain a modular monolith in production, including deployment frequency, mean time to recovery, and infrastructure-as-code adoption?
  \item How do architectural decisions affect the time and effort required for new team members to become productive, including documentation quality, local development setup, and module discoverability?
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Guideline Orientation}

Open questions in this dimension include:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=8pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item How can architectural guidance be packaged as reusable templates and code references that reduce ambiguity when applying the guidelines in practice?
  \item How should recommendations be adapted to the specific business context, team size, and growth stage of the organization?
  \item How can guidance be framed around the trade-offs and dilemmas inherent in architectural decisions rather than as prescriptive rules?
\end{itemize}

\section{Verification Approach}
\label{sec:verification-plan}

To strengthen the practical and academic relevance of the architectural guidelines proposed in this chapter, a two-instrument verification was conducted. The verification targets the six guidelines (G1--G6) through semi-structured interviews with industry practitioners and academic researchers, together with a structured post-interview questionnaire. The open research directions identified in Section~\ref{sec:future-guidelines} will be verified through the same interview-based methodology in subsequent work, with organizational case studies added as appropriate to the dimension under study.

Participants included software engineering practitioners from industry and researchers from academia, providing both practice and research perspectives. All participants provided verbal informed consent at the start of the recording, covering the study's purpose, data use, and right to withdraw. Recordings were stored securely with restricted access, and transcripts were anonymized before analysis, following standard research ethics practice.

\subsection*{Interview Method}

The primary verification method was semi-structured interviews with selected practitioners. Participants included professionals with relevant expertise in designing or evolving monolithic and microservices systems or leading software architectural decisions in startups. Each interview explored the perceived usefulness, feasibility, and completeness of the guidelines, as well as any limitations or omissions experts identified. The feedback collected guided subsequent refinements to the guidelines.

Interviews were conducted remotely and followed a flexible script structured around four thematic areas:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=8pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item General impressions and relevance of the guideline set
  \item Detailed feedback on the wording, examples, and exercises
  \item Contextual challenges in applying the guidelines in early-stage, cloud-native applications
  \item Suggestions for improving or decomposing guidelines into more actionable steps
\end{enumerate}

Participants received summarized descriptions of the guidelines in advance to facilitate discussion. With participant consent, all interviews were recorded and transcribed. Transcripts were stored securely and coded using thematic analysis.

\subsection*{Analysis Approach}

Interview transcripts were examined to identify recurring themes. The researcher verified three coding categories:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=8pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item Viability barriers (e.g., tooling limitations, organizational culture misalignment, lack of time or resources)
  \item Viability enablers (e.g., clear boundaries, simplicity of implementation, improved modularity)
  \item Gaps or missing details (e.g., unclear terminology, need for additional examples or prerequisites)
\end{enumerate}

Feedback was categorized according to the four evaluation dimensions defined in Chapter~\ref{sec:relatedwork}. The researcher cross-referenced these comments against the original literature gaps identified in the systematic review to confirm that the guidelines address underexplored needs and to inform any refinements.

Each interview was complemented by a short structured questionnaire that gathered Likert ratings on the same constructs the interview probed. The objective of combining the two instruments was methodological triangulation between the participant's qualitative narrative and their structured judgment shortly after the session, not inferential statistics on a small sample. Convergence between the two reinforced a finding; divergence was itself a finding worth probing.

The complete execution policy of the verification phase, including the interview protocol, the analysis framework, the use of artificial intelligence tools for transcription and assisted categorization, and the controls applied to the AI-assisted analysis, is consolidated in Appendix~\ref{ape:methodology}. The empirical results of the verification are synthesized in Chapter~\ref{sec:conclusion}, with individual session reports provided in Appendix~\ref{ape:interviews}.
