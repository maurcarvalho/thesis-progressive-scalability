\label{sec:conclusion}

This chapter consolidates the contributions of the dissertation, presents the empirical results of the verification phase, answers the research question, articulates the limitations of the work, and identifies the research directions that emerged from both the literature gaps and the expert interviews. The previous chapters established the empirical and conceptual foundation for examining modular monoliths as a deliberate architectural choice for early-stage cloud-native systems; this chapter closes the dissertation by synthesizing the contribution of the proposed guidelines, by reporting how they performed under expert scrutiny, and by stating what the work does not yet establish.

\section{Summary of Contributions}

This research contributes a set of architectural guidelines for software startups adopting modular monolith architectures while preserving scalability, maintainability, and internal modularity. Building on the twelve evaluation criteria synthesized from the systematic literature review across four analytical dimensions, this dissertation proposes six guidelines (G1--G6) that fully operationalize the Architectural Design and Operational Fit dimensions. Together, they form a progression in which each guideline builds on the previous one, moving from enforced modularity toward observable, extraction-ready architecture.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=8pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{G1, Enforce Modular Boundaries:} positions modularity as an enforceable property, sustained through explicit declarations, automated verification, and build-time checks.
  \item \textbf{G2, Embed Maintainability:} treats maintainability as a bounded cost of change, tracked through stable contracts, unidirectional dependencies, and structural metrics.
  \item \textbf{G3, Design for Progressive Scalability:} introduces a four-level scalability spectrum (L0 to L3) that absorbs growth through proportional, evidence-driven interventions.
  \item \textbf{G4, Promote Migration Readiness:} prepares modules for extraction inside the monolith through Anti-Corruption Layer gateways, an orchestrated saga implemented with Temporal, durable cross-module event distribution implemented with Apache Kafka, and versioned event contracts.
  \item \textbf{G5, Streamline Deployment Strategy:} defines a deployment spectrum (D0 to D2) that mirrors G3, supported by a single monorepo and module-aware CI/CD pipelines.
  \item \textbf{G6, Introduce Observability Patterns:} establishes module-scoped observability with OpenTelemetry, Prometheus, Grafana, and Jaeger that survives extraction unchanged.
\end{itemize}

The guidelines are grounded in Tiny Store, a working reference implementation built as an Nx monorepo with four bounded contexts (orders, inventory, payments, shipments). All code listings in G1 to G6 are drawn from repository source files and verified against the running system, ensuring that each principle is operationalized rather than purely conceptual.

The systematic literature review revealed that existing studies cover individual techniques such as modular boundary enforcement, maintainability patterns, migration strategies, deployment automation, and observability tooling, yet none of the eighteen reviewed articles integrate these techniques into a cohesive, progressive guideline set. The contribution of this work lies in that integration: the G1 to G6 progression synthesizes individually studied techniques into a deliberate scalability path. Schema isolation, caching, asynchronous queues, event-driven communication, and distributed tracing are positioned as architectural requirements from project inception, not as migration prerequisites added later, so that extraction becomes a deployment-topology change instead of a codebase rewrite.

A third contribution comes from the expert verification phase. Fifteen semi-structured interviews with industry practitioners and academic researchers, together with a structured post-interview questionnaire, produced refinements to G1 to G6 and a synthesis of the trade-offs, edge cases, and contextual adaptations that experts surfaced in practice. The interviews moved the guidelines from a set of hypotheses drawn from the literature to a set that has been read, questioned, and adjusted by working practitioners, completing the three-phase research design that frames this dissertation.

A fourth contribution synthesizes the six guidelines into the Extraction Decision (Section~\ref{sec:extraction-decision}), a six-question checklist that combines Khononov's Balanced Coupling signals~\cite{khononov2024balancing} with Newman's Three Key Questions~\cite{newman2019monolith}, and that separates structural readiness (a precondition supplied by G1--G6) from migration recommendation (an outcome-driven decision). A worked application to the Tiny Store payments and inventory modules illustrates how the framework yields different decisions for modules that pass the same structural gates.

\section{Empirical Verification Results}

\subsection*{Participant profile and protocol adherence}

The verification phase conducted fifteen semi-structured interview sessions between 28 May and the middle of June 2026. Eleven participants identified as industry practitioners, three as mixed industry-and-academic profiles, and one as a senior academic researcher. Years of professional experience ranged from four to seventeen, with a median above ten years. The sample covered Brazilian, Australian, French, and United States contexts, and spanned modular monolith environments at scales from early-stage startups to industrial-scale Ruby on Rails monoliths with thousands of engineers. Each session lasted between forty-five and eighty minutes and followed the seven-question protocol consolidated in Appendix~\ref{ape:methodology}, with the first session preceding the protocol revision and producing a coverage imbalance in the deferred dimensions, which was corrected in subsequent sessions. A structured post-interview questionnaire (Appendix~\ref{ape:methodology}) was sent within twenty-four hours of every session and was returned by a majority of participants within the agreed one-week window.

\subsection*{Per-guideline refinements}

\paragraph*{G1 Enforce Modular Boundaries.} G1 was reaffirmed across every session as the most defensible operational guideline and as the natural entry point of the framework. The verification surfaced two refinements. First, the application framework itself can become a structural obstacle: Ruby on Rails autoloaders and automatic imports make module boundaries invisible at the language level, requiring external tooling or framework modifications to enforce the guideline. Second, the language of the underlying stack affects the practicality of boundary tooling, with strongly-typed and explicit-import languages providing native support that dynamically-typed languages lack. The G1 narrative remains intact; the paper benefits from acknowledging these obstacles.

\paragraph*{G2 Embed Maintainability.} G2 was confirmed as the maintainability layer of the framework with one strong refinement: G2 adoption is gated by team maturity, not by technical complexity. Mature teams adopt the metric set naturally; less experienced teams ignore it even when documented. Two further refinements emerged. First, metric definitions are framework-dependent and require normalization or framework-specific guidance to operationalize. Second, code semantics and naming, framed within Domain-Driven Design vocabulary, deserve treatment as a concrete maintainability mechanism that the current G2 narrative underweights. A direct outcome of the verification phase is the introduction of the Integration Strength Index (ISI), derived from Khononov's Integration Strength taxonomy~\cite{khononov2024balancing}, which Waldemar (Interview 04) identified as the canonical reference for software coupling metrics. The ISI is added to the G2 metric set in Section~\ref{sec:g2-embed-maintainability}.

\paragraph*{G3 Design for Progressive Scalability.} G3 received the strongest consolidated affirmation as the conceptual organizing axis of the framework. The verification produced two refinements. The Sync Ratio target of thirty percent is recognized in practice but its origin should be justified with a literature reference. The in-memory event pattern was confirmed as a viable entry-level mechanism but requires explicit acknowledgment of state-management considerations, since teams without a robust state-management discipline can produce fragile event-driven implementations within the monolith.

\paragraph*{G4 Promote Migration Readiness.} G4 produced a mixed signal in the verification. One participant proposed removing sagas from the paper to avoid biasing toward distributed-systems thinking. Two participants proposed broadening the catalog to include state machines, rewrite-in-place, and in-memory orchestration as situational alternatives. One participant explicitly defended sagas as the practice with the highest immediate operational value, framed around process reversibility and toil reduction. The remaining sessions were neutral or did not engage with the G4 narrative. The cross-session signal supports refinement, not displacement: sagas remain the central mechanism within G4, with alternative patterns acknowledged as context-dependent choices. The dissertation's pro-saga position is therefore reinforced, not diluted, by the orchestrated and choreographed catalog now formalized in Section~\ref{sec:g4-saga-styles}: sagas remain the recommended starting point for the target profile, with the choreographed alternative documented as a situational choice for multi-team contexts where it becomes preferable.

\paragraph*{G5 Streamline Deployment Strategy.} The D0 to D2 deployment spectrum was confirmed as a useful framing. The verification produced a concrete operational example for D2 in pod-based deployment isolation at industrial scale, where domain groups deploy as independent units while sharing the same monorepo, with customer-tier routing handled at the infrastructure layer. One participant raised the question of whether the deployment spectrum is better treated as an infrastructure concern (handled by load balancers and service meshes) than as a code-level architectural commitment.

\paragraph*{G6 Introduce Observability Patterns.} G6 produced the strongest consolidated signal of the verification. Every participant who addressed the operational dimension prioritized observability, in different framings but in the same direction: observability is the cross-cutting prerequisite of the framework. The verification produced two refinements. First, the cross-domain trace propagated through correlation identifiers is the differential affordance of G6 in a modular monolith, not the per-module metric in isolation; the paper incorporates this refinement in Section~\ref{sec:g6-introduce-observability-patterns}. Second, observability brings value whether or not upstream guidelines are in place and should not be gated by the composite gate logic that ties later guidelines to the success of earlier ones.

\subsection*{Cross-cutting themes}

The five saturation axes declared in Appendix~\ref{ape:methodology} produced the following cross-session status. The articulation of the \emph{Enforcement Gap} as a named concept was spontaneous in the first session and was reaffirmed in substance by the majority of subsequent sessions, with the political-barriers framing offered in the most recent sessions as an organizational reformulation of the same underlying difficulty. The \emph{rigid-boundary, free-interior} distinction was implicit in most sessions through the consistent endorsement of Domain-Driven Design vocabulary as the operational language of G1 and G2. The \emph{pre-extraction sagas as practice} axis is the only one that produced a contested signal, with the majority contesting the centrality of sagas and a single voice defending them as immediate-value. The axis of \emph{observability as systemic prerequisite} is fully consolidated, with every session reaffirming it. The axis of \emph{code listings as principle versus prescription} was addressed by a minority of sessions; the strongest signal was that the original listings read as more prescriptive than intended, justifying the principle-versus-prescription framing already adopted in the paper.

Three cross-cutting themes emerged that do not map cleanly to the five saturation axes. The first is a structural critique of the four-dimension framework: three independent participants proposed that Guideline Orientation behaves as a premise or maturity axis that permeates the other three dimensions rather than as a parallel dimension, with one explicit proposal of a staged model in which the guidelines are introduced in phases corresponding to product, team, and operational maturity. The second is the dual nature of AI coding assistants: while one participant proposed an AI agent for a single guideline as a concrete future research direction, another described the same tooling as a source of architectural drift that reduces the room for non-standard refactorings and reinforces framework defaults. The third is the appearance of organizational politics as a first-order obstacle to modularization, framed in one session as the most binding constraint independently of the architectural style adopted; this finding reinforces the case for treating the Organizational Alignment dimension as substantive research rather than as a deferred placeholder, and aligns with the practitioner literature that frames organizational challenges as more difficult than the technical ones in microservice adoption~\cite{vitharana2024challenges}.

\subsection*{Triangulation with the structured questionnaire}

The post-interview questionnaire is the second instrument of the verification and is used as a triangulation device, not as a basis for inferential statistics on a small sample. The questionnaire was returned by the majority of participants; medians on the per-guideline ratings stayed at four or five out of five across most dimensions, with two notable exceptions. The G4 clarity dimension received the only sub-four rating produced by a respondent who had recommended removing sagas from the paper, reaffirming the qualitative critique. The composite binary gates mechanism received the lowest single-respondent rating from the participant who questioned the sequential dependency assumption in the conversation, again converging with the qualitative signal. Two participants treated the ranking grid as a Likert-style intensity scale instead of a strict ordinal rank, a limitation addressed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations} below. Both optional reflection fields in Section~7 of the questionnaire produced material that reinforced the qualitative findings of the respective sessions, including the staged-model proposal already cited under the cross-cutting themes.

The convergence between the qualitative narrative and the structured judgment is strongest on the G6 strength, the contested G4 framing, and the G1 foundational role. The divergences that surfaced are themselves findings: they highlight cases where the participant's conceptual endorsement does not match their forced-ranking choice for an early-stage team, and these tensions are documented per appendix instead of being averaged away.

Seven additional respondents returned the structured questionnaire after reading the pre-read paper independently, without participating in the semi-structured interview. These responses constitute a second verification instrument distinct from the interview series: the role is to provide an independent quantitative check on the framework's reception by practitioners who engaged with the paper without the conversational anchoring of an interview. The aggregate analysis is reported in Appendix~\ref{ape:questionnaire-only}; the headline is convergence on the framework's structural choices (the general framing, the per-guideline ratings, the spectrum and gate mechanisms) with three substantive divergences that each reinforce a qualitative theme from the interview series: a lower G6 adoption likelihood that converges with the cost-conditioned and timing-based critiques of Interviews 12, 13, and 14, a shift in the $\varepsilon_m$ calibration toward \emph{too strict} that converges with the soft-gates reformulation proposed in Interview 12, and a higher G7 Team-Architecture Balance priority that converges with the Organizational Alignment cross-axis reformulation surfaced in Interviews 9, 10, and 13. The global statements on sample size in this chapter and elsewhere remain anchored on the fifteen-session interview series; the complementary instrument is reported as additional triangulation, not as additional interview data.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection*{Answer to the Research Question}

The dissertation set out to answer the question of which architectural aspects must be evaluated, as monolithic applications evolve, to preserve modularity and ensure distribution-readiness in order to avoid premature adoption of microservices. This research identifies six such aspects, organized in two dimensions of the proposed analytical framework. Within Architectural Design, modular boundaries must be enforced rather than assumed (G1), maintainability must be embedded through metrics and not added later (G2), and scalability must be designed as a progressive path instead of a single jump (G3). Within Operational Fit, modules must be prepared for extraction through anti-corruption layers and cross-domain consistency mechanisms (G4), the deployment pipeline must evolve alongside the architecture (G5), and observability must be module-scoped and present from day one, not added at extraction (G6).

Each of the six aspects is grounded in the literature review of Chapter~\ref{sec:relatedwork}, operationalized through the Tiny Store reference implementation of Chapter~\ref{sec:proposal}, and examined against expert practice in the interviews reported above.

The Architectural Design chapter closes by synthesizing the six aspects into the Extraction Decision framework (Section~\ref{sec:extraction-decision}), which makes explicit the question that G1--G6 implicitly assume: \emph{when} a module should be extracted, not only whether it is structurally ready. The framework binds the readiness gates of G3 and G4 to outcome-driven criteria that the monolith cannot deliver, separating the prescriptive layer (the guidelines) from the decision layer (the framework). The expert verification produced two refinements that the paper now incorporates: cross-domain traceability as the differential affordance of G6, and the orchestrated and choreographed saga catalog of Section~\ref{sec:g4-saga-styles}, which formalizes the request that sagas be presented as one option among several mechanisms for cross-domain consistency. The verification also surfaced three reformulation proposals that the current framework does not commit to but that are recorded for post-defense iteration: a staged-model in which G1 to G6 are organized as progressive phases of product, team, and operational maturity instead of six parallel guidelines; the reframing of Guideline Orientation as a maturity axis instead of a parallel dimension; and the relaxation of the sequential dependency in the composite gates so that observability can be adopted whether or not upstream guidelines are mature.

\subsection*{Reframing proposals surfaced during verification}

The verification produced four reformulation proposals. Three are acknowledged as findings without committing the current framework to them, and one has been incorporated. The first is the staged-model proposal, in which G1 to G6 are organized as progressive phases corresponding to product, team, and operational maturity instead of six parallel guidelines. The second is the structural critique of the four-dimension framework, in which Guideline Orientation is recast as a premise that permeates the other three dimensions. The third is the widening of the G4 catalog to acknowledge orchestrated and choreographed sagas as situational choices instead of presenting a single saga style; this proposal has been incorporated through the catalog of Section~\ref{sec:g4-saga-styles}, which now develops both styles with an explicit decision rule. The fourth is the relaxation of the sequential dependency in the composite gates, with observability promoted to a cross-cutting prerequisite that does not require upstream maturity to deliver value.

The three proposals not yet incorporated are recorded in the per-session appendices and referenced here as directions for the post-defense iteration of the framework. They do not invalidate the current G1 to G6 set; they suggest reformulations of how the same six guidelines could be presented and adopted as the framework matures into a second-generation artifact.

\subsection*{Implications for practice}

For early-stage cloud-native engineering teams, the practical implications of this research can be summarized as follows. First, modularity must be enforceable from day one through automated checks and not assumed through good intentions; the tooling investment required to operationalize G1 is non-negligible and should be planned alongside the first delivery milestones. Second, observability is the highest-return investment in the operational layer and should be present from project inception, including correlation identifiers that propagate across module boundaries, so that the team can debug and reason about the system as it grows. Third, sagas and cross-domain consistency mechanisms should be adopted on the basis of context, not on the basis of architectural fashion; for early-stage teams without distributed-systems experience, simpler mechanisms such as state machines or in-process event handling often deliver the same operational value at lower cognitive cost~\cite{newman2019monolith}. Fourth, the deployment pipeline should be allowed to evolve alongside the architecture, starting from a single artifact and progressing to module-aware build scoping as the system grows, without forcing the team into a multi-artifact topology before evidence justifies it. Fifth, the use of AI coding assistants should be approached with awareness of their dual nature: the same tooling that lowers the cognitive barrier of adopting the guidelines may also reinforce framework defaults and reduce the room for non-standard architectural discipline.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The limitations of this verification are reported across four areas: the instrument and the protocol, the researcher's role, the sample and its scope, and the analysis.

\subsection*{Instrument and protocol}

Three considerations matter on the instrument and the protocol. The first is the alignment between the protocol questions and the constructs the verification intends to probe. The first session used an earlier thirty-question script that proved too long for the available time budget and produced predominant coverage of the Architectural Design and Operational Fit dimensions at the expense of Organizational Alignment and Guideline Orientation. The protocol was revised to seven questions with one dedicated to the deferred dimensions for the subsequent sessions; the imbalance of the first session is documented in Section~\ref{sec:interview01}. The second is the influence of the pre-read material on participant articulations: every participant received the paper before the session, which may have anchored their feedback on the paper's vocabulary. The protocol revision defers technical nomenclature until after open responses to mitigate this risk; the residual effect is documented per session. The third is the use of artificial intelligence tools for transcription and assisted categorization. The execution policy declared in Appendix~\ref{ape:methodology} prescribes three controls: the original recording is the source of truth for all quotations, every verbatim quotation is verified word by word against the transcript, and AI-proposed categorizations are checked against the original conversation and not against AI-generated summaries.

\subsection*{Researcher role}

Researcher bias is the dominant concern. The interviewer is the author of the proposed framework; this creates a known risk of leading questions and confirmation bias. The protocol mitigates this through the inversion question (Q7: \emph{``if you were reviewing this paper for a journal rather than participating in the interview, what would your single biggest reservation be?''}), which is designed to elicit reservation that participants tend to soften in conversation with the author. A second concern is the selection of participants through personal and professional networks: the recommendation channels are biased toward environments aligned with the dissertation's target architectural pattern, which may over-represent convergence. The cross-session findings that contradict the framework, such as the contested G4 catalog and the proposed structural reformulations, suggest that the selection bias did not suppress critique, but the limitation is acknowledged.

\subsection*{Sample and scope}

Generalization from the verification sample to the population of cloud-native software startups is limited. The sample of fifteen participants is small by inferential-statistics standards and was chosen for thematic depth, not for statistical representativeness; the verification does not claim generalizable conclusions about cloud-native startups as a population. The participants span Brazilian, French, American, and Australian contexts but cluster around the cloud-native engineering tradition rooted in the Ruby, Rails, and Node.js ecosystems; environments operating under different ecosystems (Java enterprise, .NET, embedded systems) are not represented and the framework's applicability to those contexts is not established by this verification. The sample also clusters around organizations either operating modular monoliths or migrating toward them, which is methodologically appropriate for the dissertation's scope but means the verification does not directly engage with practitioners committed to alternative architectural styles.

\subsection*{Analysis and measurement}

On the analysis side, the main observation is that the work does not make inferential statistical claims. The structured post-interview questionnaire is used as a triangulation device whose convergence with the qualitative narrative is reported as a finding, not as a statistical signal; the small sample precludes hypothesis testing. Two participants treated the ranking grids of the questionnaire as Likert-style intensity scales instead of strict ordinal ranks, a measurement-level concern that the reporting acknowledges per session. The thematic coding categories declared in Appendix~\ref{ape:methodology} (viability enablers, viability barriers, and gaps or missing details) were applied consistently across the fifteen sessions, with the categorization of every finding verified against the original conversation as described above under the instrument and the protocol.

\section{Future Research}

The future research agenda of this work is shaped by both the literature gaps identified in Chapter~\ref{sec:relatedwork} and the directions surfaced during the expert verification. Four categories of future work are identified.

The deferred analytical dimensions of the framework, Organizational Alignment and Guideline Orientation, are the natural continuation of the dissertation's research program. The verification reinforced both as substantive directions rather than as residual categories: organizational politics emerged as a first-order obstacle to modularization independently of the architectural style adopted, a finding that the broader practitioner literature treats as a defining feature of microservice adoption rather than a peripheral concern~\cite{vitharana2024challenges}, and the four-dimension framing of Guideline Orientation was challenged by three independent voices proposing reformulations. The research questions opened in Section~\ref{sec:future-guidelines} of Chapter~\ref{sec:proposal} remain open and now have empirical grounding from the verification sessions.

The most promising route to practical evaluation of the guidelines is to embed them into the tools developers already use every day. A Ruby on Rails extension that treats modules as native architectural units, with built-in boundary enforcement, module-scoped configuration, and extraction-ready conventions, would expose the guidelines to a large practitioner community while preserving the ergonomics that make Rails the dominant choice for early-stage startups. Beyond framework integration, language-specific libraries and AI coding assistants offer a complementary path, surfacing boundary, scalability, and extraction-readiness checks at development time. The verification surfaced a tooling stack proposed by one participant as the practical adoption layer: a dependency graph generator analogous to existing module-boundary tools, a structured logger with automatically injected domain metadata, and metrics that count cross-domain calls. Such a stack would move the modularity discipline from a problem of developer vigilance to a problem solved by the tooling itself, and is recorded here as a future-work direction.

The dissertation's scope excludes empirical evaluation at the production-grade scale. The expert verification indicates the perceived viability and records the structural critiques of the framework but does not measure the impact of the guidelines on a real codebase over time. A natural extension is a longitudinal case study in which a modular monolith adopts G1 to G6 from inception, measures the verification metrics at regular intervals, and reports the effect on the cost of change, the extraction readiness, and the operational observability over multiple years. This extension would close the loop between literature, prescription, and measured outcome.

Security and DevSecOps integration is the fourth open direction. Unlike the previous three, this axis did not emerge from the verification phase or the systematic literature review; it was raised by the dissertation proposal defense as a complementary axis relevant to the target profile of cloud-native startups, and is reported here to acknowledge that strategic feedback. The current framework treats security implicitly through the boundary enforcement of G1 and the contract discipline of G2, but does not address authentication boundaries, secret management, or the security implications of extracting a module across a network boundary where in-process trust no longer holds. A natural extension is a security-oriented companion to the framework that examines how each of G1 to G6 interacts with the DevSecOps practices that early-stage cloud-native teams adopt alongside the architectural discipline, including how the Extraction Decision should incorporate threat-modeling outcomes as an additional question on the checklist.

\section{Final Remarks}

This dissertation treats architectural guidance as an evolving construct, shaped by both empirical evidence and contextual complexity. The central argument of this work, that modular monoliths can achieve progressive scalability through deliberate, evidence-driven architectural interventions without premature distribution, is operationalized through six guidelines, embodied in a working reference implementation, and examined through a first expert verification that indicates viability. The verification refined the guidelines, surfaced reformulation proposals that the framework records as future-work directions, and reinforced the case for treating Organizational Alignment as substantive research instead of a deferred placeholder. The most promising route to continued evaluation is to embed the guidelines into the tools developers already use every day, so that progressive scalability stops being an architectural intention and becomes a default of the development environment itself.
